# The Sword

A sword lying flat across the sky, point west and hilt east, level from end to end. The star at its point is the brightest star there is.

It is a monument to weapons being put down, and it is shaped like a weapon. That is not an oversight.

## The figure

Six stars and five lines: a long blade running east to west, a crosspiece at the hilt end, and a pommel beyond it.

What makes it read is that it does not tilt. The figure runs from declination nineteen to thirty-two, thirteen degrees of variation across twenty-four degrees of sky, which for a thing twice as long as it is tall is as close to level as the stars allow. Every other weapon-figure in this sky is held, carried or thrown. This one is on a table.

From Aru'Mas it rides high — between sixty-nine and eighty-two degrees, so it passes almost overhead and stays there, lying across the top of the night rather than rising out of anything.

The suns stand in its sign from **40 Embrathen** to **27 Lochenvir**, thirty days, though they pass a good fourteen degrees south of the blade itself. It stands highest at midnight around **2 Ossandrel**.

## Piercers Point

The star at the tip is magnitude −1.49. There is nothing brighter in the sky.

It is called **Piercers Point**, and it is the oldest-feeling thing about the figure, because it is a name about what a sword is for attached to the one part of a laid-down sword that still has an edge. Whoever named the star was not being sentimental about peace. They were looking at the sharpest thing in the heavens and calling it what it was.

The star would be noticed whatever shape was drawn around it. It is the first star anyone learns, and from here it climbs to eighty-two degrees. A figure built outward from it was always going to be a figure people could find.

## The weapons laid down

The sign is a reminder of the weapons set aside between the spirits and the people who came here, and a promise that war is to be avoided at all costs.

That is the whole of the reading, and it is phrased as a promise rather than as a fact — which is the correct tense. The [[First Pact]] ended thirteen years of fighting. It did not end the conditions that caused it, and it has been tested since, in ways the city would rather be remembered for surviving than for causing.

So the figure is not a victory. Nobody won the arrangement it commemorates. It is an agreement that holds because both sides keep choosing to let it, and the sky says so by showing the thing disarmed rather than broken: the sword is intact, it is simply not in anyone's hand. It could be picked up. That is the point of drawing it whole.

## At all costs

The phrase is the uncomfortable part and it is meant to be.

*Avoided at all costs* is not the language of a people who found peace easy. It is the language of people who know exactly what the alternative costs, because they paid it once and are explicit that they will pay a great deal to avoid paying it again. A promise with "at all costs" in it has already conceded that the costs will be real and that they will be borne.

The figure is read at oath-takings and at the renewal of standing agreements, and that sentence is the part that gets said. It is a strange thing to hang over a city — an intact weapon, point-first, in the brightest star in the sky, meaning *not this, whatever it takes*.

It is also, of the thirteen figures, the only one that is an instruction.
