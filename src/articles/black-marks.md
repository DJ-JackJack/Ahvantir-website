# Black Marks

Black Marks are a set of identifying markings, simple enough to be overlooked by a casual
observer, yet clear to those with a discerning eye. Used largely by the [[Looming Grasp]] and its
sub-agencies now, the black marks have been with Aru'Mas' criminals since the first cut purse
took to its streets. The marks are a shorthand, identifiers that pass along covert information.

**The Fool** marks a target whose ideals, trust, or naivety lend them to being easily taken
advantage of. This marking tells a prospective criminal that the marked is a good target, but
also that they should tread carefully so as not to remove the target as a resource for future
criminals. **The Knife** denotes a target who has double-crossed or broken a deal with another
operative; it tells the criminal that they are not to be trusted and that there may be a reward
available if the marked one is dealt with.

## What a Mark Says

No mark is a label. Every one of them is an appraisal joined to an instruction, and the
instruction is what the vocabulary exists to carry. The Fool says *worth taking*, and then says
*do not break him*. The Knife says *worthless as a partner*, and then says *there may be coin in
him*.

The Fool's restraint is not mercy. It is conservation of prey. A mark that says "easy, and leave
him standing" protects a revenue stream for criminals the marker will never meet and would not
help if he did.

That is the whole logic of the system, and the reason outfits that despise one another honour the
same vocabulary. The Grasp, the [[Dragon's Claws]], the [[Iron Boots]] and every freelancer
working out of [[Ragmarket]] all benefit from a shared map of who can be bled and who will get
you killed. The marks are infrastructure. They are a road everybody uses.

Which makes the gravest offence in the system not reading marks you were not meant to read, but
**writing a false one**.

## Reading a Wall

A mark alone says very little. Five layers of inflection carry the rest.

**The mark.** The vocabulary item. What is being said.

**The medium.** Tense. Chalk and charcoal mean *current* — someone is working this now, and a
chalk mark still legible in a month means somebody died or fled. Harbour pitch means *standing*,
reaffirmed by whoever last renewed it. Cut or scratched — into a lintel, a mooring post, the
mortar between cobbles — means *settled*, a fact about this person or place. A cut mark is a
verdict. A chalk mark is a rumour. A chalk mark laid over a cut one that contradicts it is an
argument in progress.

**The modifier.** Struck through means cancelled: debt paid, bounty collected, information
expired. Doubled means urgent or high value. Inverted means the opposite of the mark's plain
sense. Ringed or boxed means authorised, which is a house convention rather than a universal one —
within the Grasp it marks a standing order from a Veiled Hand rather than a runner's own judgement,
and to an outfit that does not work that way the ring is decoration.

**The name.** Who says so. Rare, costly, and never written (see **Names**, below).

**Position.** Height says who the mark is for. Knee height, where it would only be seen by
someone crouched as if to tie a boot, is runners' work. Eye height is meant for everybody,
including the people it is about. Orientation says *which way* — several marks are meaningless
without it.

Five axes compose sentences. A cut Eye at eye height, pointing across the lane at a Counter,
struck through, with a name beneath it, reads: *that fence was watched, it is not any more, and I
am the one telling you so.*

## The Marks

Every mark must survive a casual glance as a chisel slip, a mason's tally, a child's scratch or
rust. A mark that looks deliberate has failed before it has been read.

### People

| Mark | Appraisal | Handling |
|---|---|---|
| **The Fool** | Ideals, trust or naivety make them easy | Take, but leave them standing. They are a resource. |
| **The Knife** | Broke a deal with an operative | Do not trust. There may be a reward if they are dealt with. |
| **The Hook** | Buyable, and knows exactly what they are | Approach direct, bring coin, assume they will sell you to the next buyer. |
| **The Collar** | Owed to the Grasp through debt | Do not rob, harm or frighten. A faucet, not a purse. |
| **The Dry Well** | Looks fat, is empty. Already bled | Spend your time elsewhere, and know the Legion may still be watching it. |
| **The Open Mouth** | Talks to the [[The Sunspear Legion]] | Nothing said in front of them, nothing sold to them. |
| **The Bent Spear** | A Legionnaire who takes coin | Approachable, at a price, and never in daylight. |
| **The Scar** | The [[Hand of Throk]] is working this | Expect interference, expect no killing, expect them to be better at it than you. |
| **The Net** | Bait. Somebody is watching | Walk past. |
| **The Deep** | Beyond you | Do not approach. Do not test it. Go elsewhere. |
| **The Roof** | Under the marker's protection | Touch them and you have not robbed a stranger. You have insulted whoever signed it. |

**The Deep** is the only mark in the vocabulary that appraises the marker rather than the target.
It is a confession: *I looked at this and walked away.* It deliberately does not say what the
danger is. It covers a [[Spirit Pacts|Blood Debt]] heavy enough to bleed onto whoever stands
nearby, someone simply far stronger than the marker, someone protected by a house or an outfit
that cannot be seen, someone who killed the last man who tried, and something that is not a
person at all wearing one. The refusal to distinguish is the point. A mark that explained itself
would require the marker to have understood what they ran from, and the ones who understood did
not come back to carve anything.

**The Roof** is the only mark that is signed, and the only one that spends the system's central
protection. Everything else is anonymous, which is the architecture: an unsigned scratch
implicates nobody, and the Grasp's one rule is *Do Not Get Caught*. An anonymous threat deters no
one, so the Roof buys deterrence by accepting attribution. The marker is signing a wall in a city
whose Legion is actively hunting people who do that. It follows that only somebody whose
reputation outweighs their anonymity can afford to place one. For a street runner, putting their
own name beside a Roof is closer to a confession than a boast.

A Roof has three states. Signed, it is real protection with a real cost attached to ignoring it.
Unsigned, it is a bluff, and the street reads it as a joke or an insult and will test it to find
out which. **Struck through, it is protection withdrawn** — delivered by erasure rather than
announcement, which means the person it protected may be the last to learn of it.

### Places

| Mark | Appraisal | Handling |
|---|---|---|
| **The Open Door** | Shelter. Will not turn you in | Knock the way you were taught. Do not arrive hot. |
| **The Counter** | Business, no questions | Bring goods, not problems. This one charges. |
| **The Gap** | A way through — loose grate, unbarred window, a wall that takes a boot | Directional. The most valuable mark in the language to a man being chased. |
| **The Eye** | Watched | Directional, or useless. Assume you are seen from the moment you pass it. |
| **The Ash** | Burned. Raided, taken, finished | Do not come back. Warn whoever you came with. |
| **The Long Table** | Neutral ground. Crews meet here and do not bleed | Under somebody's enforcement, most often the [[Iron Boots]]. |

**The Deep** serves for places as readily as for people. A vocabulary that grew on its own would
not mint a second word for the same warning.

### Cargo

The Counter says *business here*. It does not say what, and the trade runs on specifics.

| Mark | Cargo |
|---|---|
| **The Crystal** | [[Crackle]]. The Grasp is the city's primary distributor, so this means supply, and it means somebody's leash. |
| **The Spark** | Arcane contraband — artefacts, Key Sigils for [[The Arcanum Dispersal System]]. Its own risk profile, because the [[Runeguard]] answer arcane crime and answer it differently. |
| **The Paper** | Forgery. Writs, seals, records, identity. |
| **The Coin** | Counterfeit. A harder crime than Paper, and kept distinct from it. |
| **The Bottle** | Alchemical. Poisons, [[Booshroom]], anything that works on a body. |
| **The Cage** | Live cargo. |

**The Cage** does not distinguish between exotic animals and people. The same refusal to specify
that makes the Deep work is doing something uglier here: a vocabulary that has never needed to
tell those two apart tells you exactly what the trade believes it is handling.

A **Crystal** cut beside a person-mark rather than a place-mark reads differently. That one is
using, which makes them the most exploitable person on the street and already somebody's. The
overlap with the Collar is not an accident. A Crackle dependency *is* a collar, and the Grasp
manufactures them.

### Condition and Crew

| Mark | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **The Fire** | Hot right now. Wait. Chalk by nature. |
| **Mine** | My work, my target, do not duplicate. Two crews on one house is how the Legion notices a pattern. |
| **Gone to Ground** | I have run. Do not look for me, do not go to my places, do not say my name. |
| **Taken** | Somebody was arrested. |

**Taken** is the most urgent sentence the language can form. Under *Do Not Get Caught*, an arrest
means everything that person knows is now in play, and the Grasp's standing answer to that is an
assassin sent to close the loose end before it can talk.

*Cold* has no mark of its own. A struck-through Fire says it, and the economy is deliberate.

Tallies beside any mark carry number — how many guards, how much owed, how many men came. They
are the one element legible to someone who can read nothing else, which is likely why they
survived.

A tally can also carry a time, because the city supplies one.
[[The Aloysius Don'Raffian Atripho Arcanomechanical Clock Tower|the Sentry]] shows the hour on
eight faces readable across most of Aru'Mas, day or night, which means two people who have never
met can agree on a moment without speaking. A Fire or a Long Table with a tally beside it is an
appointment.

The dependency is worth noting, since it is the only part of the vocabulary that relies on
something the city built. A mark setting a time assumes both parties can see the same clock, and
the Sentry is visible across *most* of Aru'Mas rather than all of it. Its chime does not help:
that carries only through [[Upper Hearthstone]], so everywhere else the hour must be looked at
rather than heard. Where the faces cannot be seen at all, a tally reverts to counting things and
arrangements are made the older way — in person, and badly.

## Names

An operative is identified by a personal glyph, never by anything written. A written name would be
evidence, legible to the Legion, to the literate, and to the target. The street calls these
glyphs **names** regardless, which is the kind of joke a street vocabulary makes.

A name has two separable parts, and the distinction is the entire forgery problem.

**The glyph** is the shape — who the mark claims to be. **The hand** is how that particular person
cuts that shape: stroke order, depth, the angle of the blade, which stroke they always begin with.
So the question is never *is this forged*. It is **that is his mark, but is that his hand?**

Authentication is therefore a real skill held by specific people rather than a matter of looking
closely. The old fence who has seen a Veiled Hand's true name forty times can tell you the copy
was cut left-handed. It also means forging a name requires access to the person's work, not merely
a description of it. A street runner cannot forge a Veiled Hand on a whim; he would have to have
studied one.

Glyphs are a scarce resource. Simple, distinctive, fast-to-cut shapes ran out generations ago in a
city that has been doing this for four centuries, so a person's seniority can be read off the
complexity of their name. Three strokes means they claimed it early in the city's history or
inherited it from someone who did. A newcomer's name is fussy, because fussy is what was left. A
simple name appearing fresh, in a new hand, means somebody died and somebody else took their
shape, and the street will want to know whether that was succession or theft.

Nobody assigns their own. Self-assignment cannot work, since anyone could claim any shape, so a
name only means something once other people use it — which means an operative's name is whatever
others cut when they mean him, and he may have had no say in it. A fair number of people carry a
glyph that began as an insult or a joke in their first year and never came off.

## Amendment

If anyone could strike anything the vocabulary would be worthless inside a week. The convention
that holds it together is simple: **you strike your own, and you counter-mark somebody else's.**

Erasing another man's mark is an offence of the same class as forging one, because it is a lie
told by subtraction. Disputing it is legitimate, and the way to dispute it is to cut your own
contradicting mark beside his, in your own medium, with your name beneath it if you have one worth
cutting.

So walls accumulate arguments rather than being overwritten. A doorpost carrying a cut Fool, a
pitch Dry Well and a fresh chalk Fire is three people disagreeing across years about one
household, and a reader who knows the mediums can date the argument and see who spoke last. It
also produces the one accountability the system has: strike your own marks often enough and the
street stops trusting your hand.

## What Nobody Can Fix

Cut marks are permanent and buildings outlive tenants. A doorframe in Ragmarket may carry verdicts
on four generations of people who had nothing to do with one another.

No one corrects this, because no one can. Experienced readers discount by depth, taking a mark worn
smooth to mean *somebody, once* rather than *the man who lives here*. The consequence stands
anyway: **you inherit what is cut into your door.** Rent cheap in Ragmarket and you have taken on a
dead man's reputation. A family can be bled across two generations by a Fool nobody alive remembers
carving. It is also the one route by which an entirely honest person ends up marked, and the system
has neither a mechanism to correct that nor any interest in one.

## Duress

The Grasp abandons anyone who is caught and silences anyone who might talk, so an operative forced
to leave a mark has no other way to say so. The signal is **the mark cut in the wrong stroke
order** — visible in how the scratches overlap, invisible to anyone who has not watched him cut a
real one.

It is self-limiting in exactly the way such things are. A duress signal only reaches people who know
your hand, and the Grasp runs on transactional loyalty where few people have that kind of history
with anyone. The runner being leaned on has nobody who knows his hand well enough to catch it.

And the Veiled Hands know the convention too. Signalling duress in front of someone who can read it
is a worse outcome than whatever was being forced.

## No One Owns Them

The marks are older than every organisation that uses them. They predate the [[Looming Grasp]],
they predate the [[Hand of Throk]], and they predate most of the doorframes they are cut into. No
outfit holds the practice, licenses it or can revoke it. The Grasp and its sub-agencies are among
its most prominent users and nothing more, and prominence is not ownership.

What the vocabulary asks of a person is time. There is no initiation and nobody to apply to. The
marks are learned by exposure — years on the same lanes, correction from somebody impatient, the
slow noticing that the scratch beside a door was there before the trouble was. Anyone who has spent
long enough in the shadows can read them, and that includes a good many people no organisation
would have recruited: dockhands, fences' children, Legionnaires who grew up in [[Ragmarket]], the
occasional priest.

Which means nobody can standardise it either. Outfits layer their own conventions over the shared
vocabulary — the Grasp's ring for an authorised order, a crew's habit of cutting at one particular
height — and those conventions mean nothing outside the people who keep them. More consequentially,
the vocabulary **drifts**. A mark can carry a slightly different weight in Ragmarket than it does
in [[Driftmoor Haven]], and a reader who learned it in the wrong district will be confidently wrong
about a doorpost. There is no authority to appeal to and no correct version to consult.

That absence is also what makes forgery work. Mark an honest rival as a Knife and the street kills
him for you, for free, and you were never there. In a system with an owner there would be somebody
to complain to. Here the only remedy is to cut a contradicting mark beside the lie and hope your
hand carries more weight than theirs. The [[Iron Boots]], who exist to ensure disputes happen where
they are watching, take a dim view of marks that start fights nobody sanctioned.

The reverse incentive runs on the Deep. Other marks are forged *onto* people. The Deep is forged
*off* them — scrubbed, or struck through as though it had been settled — which points somebody else
at a thing that eats them. A murder with no weapon and no body in the room.

The shapes record none of this reliably. The vocabulary accumulated across four centuries from
everybody who ever coined a mark that stuck, so it has no governing logic and never had one. A
practised reader can sometimes place roughly when a mark entered use, from how worn its conventions
are and how awkwardly it sits beside its neighbours. Who coined it is almost never recoverable.

## What the Legion Sees

Nothing. The [[The Sunspear Legion]] holds no codebook and has never assembled one. There is no
office that collects marks, no file of glyphs matched to names, no officer whose duty it is to walk
a lane and read the doorframes.

Individual legionnaires are a different matter, and the distinction is worth keeping straight. One
who grew up in the right quarter learned the marks the way everybody there learns them, by
exposure, and reads them as fluently as any runner. One who did not may have worked out a mark or
two over a career — noticed the same scratch outside three houses that were later robbed, had a
guess confirmed by an informant. Neither kind of knowledge belongs to the Legion. It belongs to
the man, and it leaves with him.

To everyone else the marks are not undecipherable. They are **invisible**. A patrol passes a
doorpost carrying four verdicts in three mediums and sees weather, a mason's slip, the usual damage
of a poor district. The vocabulary's security was never cryptographic. It rests entirely on nobody
in authority having ever thought the scratches were anything at all.

Which locates the real threat precisely. Not a cracked code — a single curious officer who starts
writing down what he sees. The Grasp's standing rule being what it is, such an officer would be
treated as an emergency rather than an inconvenience.

## What Sits Beside Them

The **Trader's Token** of [[Ragmarket]] — a medallion given to those who prove trustworthy, worn
openly for access and safe passage — is the marks' exact inverse: overt, carried, earned, and
concerned with trust rather than appraisal. Ragmarket runs both systems at once. A man holding a
Token with a cut Knife on his doorpost is one of the more interesting people in the city.

The **[[Hand of Throk]]** use the marks as everyone else does, toward different ends. Their code
forbids harm to commonfolk, so they read a Fool as a warning rather than an invitation, and they
write freely: a Roof over somebody who has no protector, an Eye on a Grasp collection, a Fire
across a lane their own people should keep out of. Defacement suits them particularly — scrubbing
a Fool off a widow's doorpost, cutting a Net beside a Collar to foul a collection — being
protective, deniable, non-violent and leaving no scar, which is their motto almost verbatim.

None of that is a counter-system. It is the same vocabulary in the hands of people who want
different things from it, which is the condition of every mark on every wall in the city.
