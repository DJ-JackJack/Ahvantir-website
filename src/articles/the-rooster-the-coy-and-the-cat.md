# The Rooster, the Coy and the Cat

Three figures on the suns' road, two signs apart each, from one story that everybody knows and almost nobody can say where they learned.

It is a spirit fable. It is older than Aru'Mas, older than the [[Drorn'Duur]], and it is currently told by children in the lower districts with a hand-clap rhythm and a rude verse about the Cat that is certainly not original. That is not a corruption of the tale so much as the ordinary fate of a good one.

## The tale

There was a pond on a great house's lawn, and in the pond lived the Coy. They knew the stones of their pond and the shadows of its weed, they knew one another, and they knew that twice a day the lord of the house came out and scattered food on the water. They knew nothing else and wanted nothing else, which is the beginning of the trouble.

One afternoon a Cat came over the lawn and saw the Coy going round and round, bright and slow and entirely unguarded, and had to sit down for a moment.

When she had composed herself she came to the water's edge and spoke in her kindest voice.

"Do you see that wall, past the hedges?"

"We see it," said the Coy. "We have always seen it."

"Then you should know," said the Cat, folding her paws, "that just beyond it there is an ocean. It goes on past seeing. It is full of fish, and there is more food in it than all of you together could eat in all your lives. Should you like to look at it?"

The Coy drew together and argued. The argument did not last long, because the answer was obvious.

"Even if we wished to," they said, "we cannot. We have no legs to walk with and no lungs to breathe with. We would die before we reached the hedge."

"Yes," the Cat agreed. "That is a problem." She leaned closer to the water. "Although — I could carry you. It is no distance at all. You would hold your breath, and you would be there before you had finished holding it."

At that the Coy scattered to the far side of the pond, every one of them.

Every one but one.

"If I let you carry me," said that one, "I will see this ocean, and eat as much as I like of it. Then I will come back and tell the rest of you whether it is true."

So the Cat took the brave Coy up in her mouth, gently, and carried him away over the lawn.

Past the wall there was no ocean. There were fields, going on past seeing, and there was a hungry cat.

The next day she came back, and the Coy came up to the surface and asked what had become of their brother.

"Oh," said the Cat, "he loved the ocean so much that he could not bring himself to leave it."

The Coy were frightened. But one of them came forward and said that somebody had to go and make sure he was safe. And the Cat left with another meal.

And so it went. Not one of them went for the ocean. Every one of them went to find the one before, and the Cat ate them in the order of their affection, which was the order in which they loved each other best.

The pond grew quiet.

All this while the Rooster had been in the yard, saying nothing, watching everything. He had understood the trick from the second day. He had also understood something the Coy had not, which was that a cat who has finished the fish does not leave the yard.

So the Rooster went to the Cat and told her he had never in his life seen anything done so well, and that he did not understand how she had managed it, and would she show him — just the lifting, just how she had got them out of the water without a splash.

The Cat, who had eaten very well and had never once been admired, went to the stone lip of the pond to show him.

The last of the Coy were waiting under the surface. They had been listening to every word.

## The figures

| **The Rooster** | head high east, tail high west, standing between | overhead at midnight in Cindralis |

They stand two signs apart each, and all three are above the horizon together on ninety-eight nights of the year — which is unusual and was not easily arranged. Two figures placed six signs apart never share a sky at all. The Cat is in the middle, where the story puts her: she can be seen with the Rooster on two hundred and fourteen nights and with the Coy on ninety-eight, and the one pairing that rarely happens is the Coy and the Rooster, who in the tale have nothing to do with one another until the last line.

The Coy is the only figure in Ahvantir's sky that did not have to be adjusted to fit what it depicts. Twenty degrees long and twenty-one deep, with a mouth at one end and a forked tail at the other, it was simply already there.

## Why it needed no translating

It is tempting to hear the great house and the kept lawn and assume the tale has been dressed up in city clothes somewhere along the way. It has not, and the assumption says more about the listener than the story.

A lord who holds land and tends what lives on it is not a settler invention. It is the oldest arrangement on these islands, and it has a name: a [[Claims and Wardens|Claim]]. A spirit's authority inside its own territory approaches the absolute, and a domain may be as small as a single hearth. A pond with a wall around it and something that feeds the creatures in it twice a day is not a borrowed image at all. It is an ordinary description of a small Claim held inside a larger one, and it was ordinary long before anyone built Aru'Mas.

So the story did not have to be moved into a new world to go on making sense. The [[Drorn'Duur]] recognised the arrangement because they lived under one. Aru'Mas recognised it because it lives under one now. What each generation of tellers adds is not new furniture but a new assumption about whose house it is — a child in the lower districts hears *a great house* and pictures the ones up on the hill, and has never once wondered whether the tale might not be about people.

And the original has not been lost. Spirits do not revise themselves, so the first telling can be had by asking one. Nobody asks. That is not scholarship failing; it is a story being too well known to check.

What survives is the shape, and the shape is the point. A stranger offers something that cannot be checked. The first one goes out of courage. Every one after goes out of love for the one before. And the creature doing the eating is not hiding — she is sitting on the lawn in daylight, being pleasant, and answering every question she is asked.
