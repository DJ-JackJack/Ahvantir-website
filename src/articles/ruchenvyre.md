# Ruchenvyre

The Ruchenvyre is among the most beloved of Ahvantir's Spirits of the Land. Where it roams freely, grain swells past its season, livestock grow fat and healthy, and a persistent sense of warmth and rightness settles over the communities that share its territory. To encounter a Ruchenvyre in the wild — a flash of gold between the roots, the smell of fresh bread where there is no hearth, a heaviness in the air like sun on warm stone — is considered one of the greatest signs of fortune the land can offer.

Those who know the Ruchenvyre's nature treat it with deep respect, not out of fear, but out of genuine affection. Unlike many Wardens, it asks for very little: modest offerings, honest labor, the keeping of the land. In return, it gives with a generosity that can feel almost embarrassing.

This is what makes its corruption, when it comes, so devastating. The Ruchenvyre does not turn malevolent because it is cruel. It turns because it is in pain.

## Appearance

The Ruchenvyre rarely manifests a stable physical form. When it does, witnesses describe something warm and round and difficult to look at directly — a shape like a great boar or a heavy dog, with hide or fur the color of ripe wheat and eyes like sunlight through honey. It does not move like a predator. It moves like something that has never had to be afraid.

In moments of deep distress or captivity, the appearance degrades. The golden hue yellows to the color of old fat. The eyes go flat. What was soft and warm becomes something that presses too heavily on the air around it, the way a smell reaches you just before you realize what it is.

When it has been fed human sacrifice, witnesses universally describe the same thing: the Ruchenvyre *shudders*, and then it *opens*, and the gold comes out wrong.

## Nature and Behavior

The Ruchenvyre is a Warden tied to agricultural land — fields, pastures, orchards, the margins where settled country meets the wilder world. Its Claim is the land it tends, and within that Claim it operates with a quiet authority. Pests do not thrive. Disease passes quickly. Weather, while not entirely in its gift, seems to find a reasonable compromise.

It is not intelligent in the way mortals are. It does not negotiate or make speeches. It communicates through the land itself: a fruitful year means it is pleased; a mild blight on a particular plot might mean the offering was skipped. Communities that have lived alongside a Ruchenvyre for generations develop an intuitive grammar for it, reading the health of their fields the way they might read a neighbor's expression.

The Ruchenvyre is profoundly indifferent to mortal politics. It does not care who rules the land so long as the land is cared for. Noble houses have learned, sometimes the hard way, that evicting the farming families a Ruchenvyre has bonded with tends to produce harvests that year that would be described, charitably, as educational.

## Offerings and Pacts

Traditional offerings to a Ruchenvyre are simple and practical: the first loaf of bread from a new batch of flour, a bundle of grain tied with red thread at the corner of a field, the first eggs of spring left at the edge of the wood. Some communities pour a small measure of oil or honey into the earth at planting time, speaking no formal prayer — addressing it the way they might address someone working alongside them in the next row.

Formal pacts with a Ruchenvyre are unusual. The relationship is more typically one of long custom — an unspoken arrangement that persists through generations — than of explicit negotiation. Where a formal pact does exist, it is usually recorded somewhere in a village's oldest papers, establishing obligations on both sides and the specific form of the spirit's favor (a guaranteed frost-free month, freedom from a particular blight, that kind of thing).

Breaking such a pact, or persistently failing to leave offerings, results in a gradual cooling of the spirit's favor. Harvests decline to merely normal. Then below normal. The Ruchenvyre does not curse the land directly; it simply stops blessing it, and the natural difficulty of farming asserts itself.

## Corruption: Capture

A Ruchenvyre that is bound — trapped within a ward, a vessel, a binding circle, or any mechanism that severs it from its Claim and holds it against its will — begins to rot.

Not quickly. Not visibly, at first. But the Claim, deprived of the spirit that tends it, starts to show strain within days. Stored food spoils faster. Livestock grow restless. The particular quality of the air the community had come to take for granted — that faint sense of rightness — goes away, and what replaces it is something subtly wrong that most people cannot name but everyone feels.

Within a fortnight, the blight becomes visible. Crops wither at the stem. Water drawn from wells carries an aftertaste. A Ruchenvyre held in captivity does not intend this — the corruption is the physical expression of its pain and severance, radiating outward through whatever connection still remains between spirit and land. It is not malice. It is the land itself, screaming.

The spirit, in captivity, becomes still. Unresponsive. It does not lash out immediately. But the stillness is not peace. Those who have spent time near a bound Ruchenvyre describe a persistent heaviness, a sense of something profoundly wrong, the knowledge that whatever you are near has a dignity that is being destroyed.

## Corruption: Sacrifice

If the captors offer the Ruchenvyre a human sacrifice — pressing the gift upon it, forcing the acceptance — something worse happens.

The Ruchenvyre cannot refuse an offering. This is part of its nature, the same nature that makes it incapable of ignoring a proper tribute of bread and oil. The forced sacrifice enters its awareness the way any offering does. And then, because what it was given is something that should not have been given, the processing of that offering goes wrong.

The spirit convulses. Witnesses describe the visible body — whatever form it has taken in captivity — shuddering as if something is being forced through it. And then it *vomits gold.*

Not metaphorically. Coins, nuggets, chains, sheets of it pour from the spirit's body. The quantity is extraordinary — more gold than the spirit's size should account for, as if something is being called from elsewhere. It pools on the ground, it splashes, it hardens. Those who have witnessed it and survived describe the sight as one of the most nauseating things they have ever seen, despite the gold's obvious value. The wrongness of it is total. One survivor described it as "watching a beloved animal vomit up something that should not be inside any living thing."

The gold itself is real and spendable. Those who handle it frequently report an unease they cannot account for, a sense of something rotten at the edges. Whether this is material or the psychological residue of witnessing the extraction, scholars at the [[The LaCroi Institute of Spiritology|LaCroi Institute]] have not determined.

Each forced sacrifice costs the Ruchenvyre something it cannot recover while in captivity. After several such events, the stillness begins to crack. What emerges from beneath it is not the warm and generous spirit of the fields. It is something that has been hurt past the point where its nature can hold.

A Ruchenvyre pushed to madness by repeated forced sacrifices begins to hunger for the thing that was pressed upon it. Not because it was good, but because pain reshapes wanting. Communities in the radius of a captured and corrupted Ruchenvyre eventually begin to lose people in ways that are difficult to account for.

## The Binding

Among those who capture Ruchenvyre for profit, a particular practice has emerged: the Binding.

A person who performs the Binding ritual — which takes approximately an hour and requires the Ruchenvyre to already be captive — ties their life-luck to the spirit's proximity. While the Bound person remains near the Ruchenvyre, they are almost impossible to harm. Weapons glance. Misfortune bounces. They feel, and those around them confirm, a persistent sense that things will go their way.

This works because the Ruchenvyre is, at its core, a spirit of prosperity. Even in captivity, even in pain, its nature leaks. The Binding redirects that leak toward a single person.

The practice is considered abhorrent by every spiritual tradition in Ahvantir. The [[Fadu-Asai]] regard it as a category of violation that demands their direct attention. The [[The Church of the Threefold Path|Church of the Threefold Path]] has issued formal condemnations. The LaCroi Institute has written extensively on why anyone who engages in it has made the worst trade available to them.

Because the moment the Ruchenvyre breaks free — and all Ruchenvyre eventually break free, or are freed, or outlast their captors — the luck reverses entirely.

A freed Ruchenvyre turns its attention to the person who performed the Binding. Not immediately, perhaps. Not loudly. But with a totality and permanence that has no parallel in recorded spirit behavior. It does not hunt them with fury. It hunts them with patience. It ignores all other threats, all other presences, all obstacles. Those who have tried to interpose themselves between a freed Ruchenvyre and its Bound target report that the spirit simply goes around them, through them, past them. Only the Bound person matters.

There are no recorded cases of a Ruchenvyre failing to eventually reach its target.

## Blood Debt and the Fabric of the Land

The capture and exploitation of a Ruchenvyre does not merely break a pact. It wounds the spiritual fabric of Ahvantir itself.

[[Spirit Pacts|Blood Debt]] is not a legal concept. It is not a penalty administered by any institution, not a judgment handed down by any authority. It is what happens when the spiritual nature of the islands — something older than the city, older than the pacts, older than the people who formalized them into custom and law — is damaged and begins to demand repair. The debt exists whether anyone acknowledges it or not. It accumulates whether anyone is watching. It will be paid whether anyone intends to pay it.

When a Ruchenvyre is captured, the land it tends registers the severance the way a living body registers infection. The rot that spreads through the Claim is not a symptom of the spirit's distress alone — it is the islands pushing back, expressing through the physical world that something has been broken that should not have been broken. The longer the captivity continues, the deeper that expression becomes.

The [[Fadu-Asai]] are drawn to such situations not because they have been assigned to them, but because they cannot ignore them. A wound of this scale in the spiritual landscape calls to them the way fire calls to water. The [[The Church of the Threefold Path|Church of the Threefold Path]] responds for the same reason — not because it governs the debt, but because it feels the pressure of it, and its people understand that the pressure will not ease until the debt is addressed. The Church has developed practices around that response over generations: free the spirit first, give it time in its Claim, maintain atonement offerings across multiple planting seasons. These are not rules. They are what observation has shown actually works.

The debt against a land does not dissolve when its human perpetrators die. It does not forgive on a timeline of mortal convenience. Those who have tried to simply outlast it have, as a rule, not fared well.
